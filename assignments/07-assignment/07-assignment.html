  1. List and briefly describe the six core components of a DeclareDesign study. Explain how these components help structure the design process.

A DeclareDesign study consists of six core components that provide a structured framework for designing, diagnosing, and improving research designs. These components help researchers clearly define each stage of their study, making the process transparent, replicable, and easier to assess for validity and power.

  1. Model – Specifies the theoretical model of the world, including assumptions about how data are generated. This includes variables, potential outcomes, and relationships between them.

  2. Inquiry – Defines the specific research question or estimand (e.g., the average treatment effect) the study aims to answer based on the model.

  3. Data Strategy – Describes how data are collected, including the sampling method and how treatments are assigned (e.g., randomization procedures).

  4. Answer Strategy – Specifies the method used to analyze the data and estimate the inquiry (e.g., regression models, difference-in-means).

  5. Diagnosands – Metrics used to evaluate the design’s performance (e.g., bias, power, RMSE). These help assess how well the design will perform under the assumed model.

  6. Design – Combines all the previous components into a complete, testable design object that can be simulated and diagnosed.

Together, these components guide researchers through building a rigorous and transparent study, ensuring each part—from theory to estimation—is logically connected and measurable.

  1. What is meant by a “true natural experiment”? Provide an example of a research scenario that could be considered a true natural experiment and explain why.

A “true natural experiment” refers to a situation in which treatment assignment occurs as if randomly, due to some naturally occurring event or external factor outside the control of the researcher or participants. This mimics the conditions of a randomized controlled trial, allowing for causal inference without deliberate intervention. The key is that the assignment mechanism must be plausibly exogenous—meaning it is unrelated to other factors that could influence the outcome.

An example of a true natural experiment is the draft lottery during the Vietnam War. In this case, U.S. men were randomly assigned draft eligibility based on their birthdates. Researchers have used this setup to study the effects of military service on later life outcomes like earnings or health. Because the birthdate-based assignment was random and unrelated to individual characteristics, it serves as a natural source of randomization, making it a strong basis for causal inference.

  1. Explain the ethical considerations that are particularly relevant when conducting quasi-experiments, even though they may arise from situations where randomisation is not ethical. Provide examples of ethical considerations that researchers should be mindful of when designing and conducting quasi-experiments.

Quasi-experiments are often used when random assignment is impractical or unethical, such as in evaluating the effects of natural disasters, policy shifts, or large-scale institutional changes. Although researchers do not control the assignment of treatment, they still have an ethical responsibility to protect the rights and well-being of participants. One major consideration is informed consent—while full experimental consent may not be possible, researchers should be transparent when collecting data, especially if it includes personal or sensitive information. Privacy and confidentiality are also essential, as quasi-experiments often rely on administrative or observational data that can inadvertently expose individuals or communities.

Researchers must also be careful not to exploit vulnerable populations, particularly when studying groups affected by hardship or crisis. For example, in a study examining the impact of a sudden school closure on student performance, researchers should avoid drawing harmful conclusions that might stigmatize communities or influence policy in negative ways. Ethical research design also includes minimizing harm, ensuring fair representation, and being cautious about how findings are communicated. Even without randomization, ethical oversight and sensitivity are vital to maintaining public trust and ensuring research benefits the populations it studies.

In this case, a quasi-experimental approach offers a better balance between ethics and evidence. Randomly assigning cities to maintain or change drug laws in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) would be ethically problematic, as it could require some cities to continue harmful policies against their will, potentially exacerbating public health issues or criminal justice inequalities. A quasi-experimental design, while less controlled, respects local autonomy and avoids deliberately imposing policies that could cause harm.

To strengthen the validity of a quasi-experimental approach, researchers can use strategies like matching, difference-in-differences, or instrumental variables to account for confounding factors. It’s also essential to be transparent about limitations, acknowledging that causal claims are more tentative than in an RCT. With rigorous analysis and ethical sensitivity, quasi-experiments can still yield valuable insights while upholding the dignity and welfare of affected communities.
